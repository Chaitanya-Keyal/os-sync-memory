CS F372 Assignment 3 - Problem 3 results
========================================

Setup
-----
VM: Ubuntu 24.04, stock kernel 6.8.0-146-generic, VirtualBox 7.2.22,
1 vCPU, 2048 MiB RAM (free reports 1968 MiB total, about 330 MiB of it
used by the system when idle). Page size 4096 B. MGLRU on, swappiness 60.
Host: Windows laptop, internal NVMe SSD, 16 GB RAM.
Swap: exactly one backing active per trial (checked by run_trials.sh),
4 GiB each. zswap disabled, no zram (swap_setup.sh does both).
  internal  /swapfile-a3, a 4 GiB swap file on the VM's root virtual disk,
            whose image lives on the host's internal NVMe SSD (host I/O
            cache off on that controller)
  usb       a 4 GiB swap area at the start of a SanDisk 3.2Gen1 64 GB pen
            drive (USB ID 0781:55a9). The whole drive is passed through to
            the VM with a VirtualBox USB filter on the xHCI controller and
            appears as /dev/sdb; swap_setup.sh wipes it and runs mkswap
            for 4 GiB.
VM settings were the same for every trial (same kernel, CPUs, RAM, USB
controller on); only the active swap backing changed. Both parts were
measured in one session by run_all.sh.
Program: memswap, seed 42, 5 passes x 10,000 lookups. Every trial is a fresh
process; run_trials.sh syncs and drops the page cache and waits 5 s before
each one, and runs the trials one at a time.
Raw output of every trial: results/raw/<backing>_<size>_<pattern>.txt.
All phases of all trials: results/trials.csv.
All 5 checksums matched in every trial.

Host note. The VM runs under Hyper-V, which pages out guest RAM the VM has
not used for a while when the Windows host is short of memory. An earlier
run, made while a browser was using several GB, showed the first trial of
each size 5-8x slower than an identical second trial even with no swapping
in the VM. So for this run the host had only the VM and a terminal open,
and run_all.sh does a throwaway 1500 MiB trial before every size so that
all guest RAM is back in host RAM before measuring. The guest-side counters
(major faults, swap KiB) are not affected by host paging either way; only
times are.

Sizes tested
  Part A, internal swap: 512, 1024, 1536, 2048, 2560 MiB, uniform and hot
  Part B, USB swap:      512, 1024, 1536 MiB, uniform and hot, plus 2048 MiB
                         (see Part B for why 2048 was added)

Findings (Part A)
1. The transition lies between 1536 and 2048 MiB. About 1.65-1.7 GiB of
   the allocation can stay in RAM. Up to 1536 MiB everything stays
   resident: no major faults, no swap traffic, lookups cost 18-56 ns
   (cache and TLB misses only). At 2048 MiB the initialization already
   writes 339 MiB to swap and every pass has thousands of major faults:
   96-152 µs per lookup for uniform, several thousand times slower than at
   1536 MiB.
2. A major fault costs about 0.5-0.9 ms from the second pass on (e.g.
   1723 ms / 3441 faults in pass 3 at 2560 MiB uniform): one 4 KiB read
   from the swap file through VirtualBox's virtual disk. The first pass is
   often dearer per fault (1.08 ms at 2560 uniform, 1.66 ms at 2048 hot):
   it also has the most swap-out (45-71 MiB), and its reads likely queue
   behind those writes. Swap-in KiB is 4 x the major faults in every pass (to
   within a few pages of background activity), so every major fault was
   one page read from swap and readahead brought in nothing extra for
   these random patterns.
3. Uniform faults match the share of the allocation that is not in RAM:
   about 1,700 of 10,000 lookups (17%) at 2048 MiB and 3,400-3,565 (34-36%)
   at 2560 MiB, close to (size - ~1.68 GiB) / size = 17% and 34%. Uniform
   has no reuse, so pass 5 has as many faults as pass 1: each page swapped
   in pushes another one out, and the resident share stays the same.
4. The hot region is slower than uniform here, the opposite of what one
   might expect, and it improves from pass to pass. Initialization touches
   pages in order, so the hot region (the first 10% of pages) is the oldest
   memory and is the first to be swapped out: after initialization the hot
   region is entirely in swap. 9,000 of the 10,000 lookups go to it, but a
   pass touches only about 13% of its 65,536 pages (at 2560 MiB), so most
   hot lookups in pass 1 fault (8725 faults vs 3565 for uniform). Each pass
   pulls more of the hot region into RAM, and the faults fall: 8725, 7556,
   6759, 5922, 5125. A simple model predicts this closely: 9,000 random
   picks from 65,536 pages hit 8,400 distinct pages, a page is still
   unread after k passes with probability e^(-0.137k), and the 1,000 cold
   lookups add ~300 faults; that gives about 8710, 7650, 6720, 5915 and
   5210. Once the 256 MiB hot region were resident, which would take about
   20 passes, the hot pattern would see almost only the ~1,000 cold lookups
   fault and would beat uniform by a wide margin. Locality pays off only
   after the working set has been brought in.
5. In the uniform trials, swap-out during the access passes happens mostly
   in pass 1 (63 MiB at 2560 MiB); later passes write 0-16 MiB. Pages that
   were swapped in and not modified keep their copy in swap (the swap
   cache), so when the kernel evicts them again it can drop them without
   writing. The hot trials keep writing 16-33 MiB per pass: pulling the
   hot region in evicts cold-region pages that were dirtied by
   initialization and have never been written to swap.
6. Allocation takes only milliseconds: posix_memalign of 1 MiB uses mmap,
   which only reserves address space. Physical pages are allocated at first
   touch, which is why initialization holds the cost (0.27-1.4 s without
   pressure, 3.7-7.5 s with pressure because it also writes 339-876 MiB
   to swap).
7. Deallocation also has 250-340 major faults under pressure. free() reads
   glibc's chunk header, which sits just before each 1 MiB block in a page
   of its own. That page was written once at allocation time and never
   again, so it is a likely candidate to be swapped out, and reading it back
   is a major fault.

Findings (Part B)
1. At 512, 1024 and 1536 MiB the backing makes no difference: neither
   backing has a single major fault or any swap traffic, so the swap device
   is never used. The small time differences (e.g. 512 uniform init 565 vs
   255 ms, 1536 uniform pass 5 0.6 vs 2.5 ms) go both ways and are noise.
   1536 MiB finished in under a second on USB, so the additional size
   between 1024 and 1536 MiB that the spec asks for when 1536 is
   impractical was not needed.
2. Because 1536 MiB never swaps, it cannot show the backing's effect. We
   therefore also ran 2048 MiB on USB, the smallest Part A size that swaps
   during the access passes; its internal side is the Part A trial.
3. Initialization at 2048 MiB is 8-12x slower on USB (34.5-46.5 s vs
   3.7-4.4 s). It writes 377-388 MiB to swap, so the pen drive sustains only
   about 8-11 MiB/s of swap writes, against at least 75-90 MiB/s for the
   internal SSD (its time also includes zeroing and filling the pages).
4. Each major fault costs 2.1-4.5 ms on USB against 0.5-0.9 ms on the
   internal SSD (from pass 2 on). Uniform pass times: pass 1 8.1 s vs 1.3 s,
   pass 5 8.3 s vs 1.0 s (6-9x). Hot: pass 1 18.7 s vs 14.0 s, pass 5
   8.5 s vs 2.1 s (4x). Hot pass 1 is the closest (1.3x) because on the
   internal SSD that pass was unusually slow per fault (Part A finding 2).
5. On USB the hot pattern's faults are cheaper (2.1-2.7 ms) than the
   uniform pattern's (2.6-4.5 ms). The hot region was swapped out together
   early in initialization, so it occupies one ~200 MiB stretch of the swap
   area; random reads confined to a small stretch are likely served faster
   by the pen drive's flash controller than reads spread over the whole
   ~400 MiB that uniform touches. The internal SSD shows no such
   difference (about 0.5-0.7 ms for both from pass 3 on).
6. Fault counts are similar on both backings (uniform 1674-1752 internal vs
   1864-2075 USB; hot 8395 -> 4242 vs 7889 -> 3984), as expected: the
   program, seed and RAM are the same, and only the cost of each fault
   changes. The small differences come from slightly different reclaim
   timing (USB initialization swapped out 397 MiB instead of 347 MiB).
   Deallocation is also slower on USB (1.1-2.0 s vs 0.3-0.6 s) because
   its 380-550 major faults are USB reads.
7. Summary: once the working set exceeds RAM, every lookup that misses
   costs a disk read, which makes it thousands of times slower than a RAM
   access even on an NVMe SSD, and a USB pen drive adds another 4-9x on
   top. The swap device only matters when there is memory pressure, and
   locality only helps once the hot pages have been brought back in.
